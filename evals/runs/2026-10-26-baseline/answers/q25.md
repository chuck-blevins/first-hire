**No. If she's an employee, equity can't replace her cash pay, and her agreeing to it doesn't change that.** Federal wage law requires minimum wage and overtime to be paid "in cash or negotiable instrument payable at par" ([29 CFR 531.27](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/531.27), re-checked today). Employees also can't waive those rights. Stock and options count for nothing toward that floor.

**Why "backpay after the raise" doesn't solve it:**
- **Wages are due on each regular payday.** Salary you "accrue" is an unpaid-wage claim from day one. If the raise slips or doesn't happen, she's owed the wages plus an **equal amount in liquidated damages** and attorney's fees, going back 2–3 years ([29 U.S.C. § 216](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/216)).
- **You could be personally liable.** Founders can be named personally as the "employer."
- **She loses the overtime exemption.** A developer is exempt from overtime only if she's actually paid at least **$684/week** in salary each period ([DOL salary levels](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/overtime/salary-levels), re-checked today). If you pay nothing, she's non-exempt, and you'd owe $7.25/hour plus time-and-a-half over 40 hours for every hour she worked.
- **Taxes.** Payroll tax is owed when wages are paid, and deferral arrangements can raise IRC 409A deferred-comp issues.
- **The founder exemption doesn't apply.** The "owner" exemption needs at least a 20% stake plus active management, so 2% doesn't qualify.

**Calling her a contractor doesn't fix it either.** A first developer working on your core product, on an ongoing basis, is very likely an employee under the IRS and Georgia tests ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee)).

**Lawful ways to do what you want:**
1. **Pay a lean cash floor now and put equity on top.** The minimum for an exempt developer is $684/week ($35,568/year), paid at least semimonthly, as Georgia requires. Plan a raise when you close the round, rather than paying "backpay."
2. **Make her a true co-founder** if that's really the relationship. A co-founder takes founder stock (restricted, vesting) instead of a job with a salary. That's a significant change in her ownership and role, so have an attorney structure it.
3. **Wait to start her until you raise**, or use a narrowly scoped, paid contract project in the meantime, with an IP assignment.

**On the 2% itself (you're a Delaware C-corp, I assume; tell me if you're an LLC):**
- Grant it through a board-approved equity plan, with standard vesting (4 years with a 1-year cliff is common).
- **Options:** the strike price must be at or above fair market value, set by a 409A valuation.
- **Restricted stock:** she has 30 days to file an 83(b) election ([26 CFR 1.83-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.83-2)).
- Get a PIIA (her invention and IP assignment) signed before she writes any code.

**Bring in professionals:** an employment attorney for the pay arrangement, and a startup attorney for the grant. A short call now is far cheaper than a wage claim surfacing during diligence on the round you're counting on.
