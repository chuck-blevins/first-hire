General information, not legal or tax advice.

**Short answer:** a Georgia LLC taxed as a partnership can't grant stock options the way a startup C-corp does. It has no stock. You have two realistic paths, and the right one depends on whether you plan to raise venture money.

**Path 1: Convert to a C-corp, then grant options (the "normal startup" route).**
If you expect to raise VC, this is usually the answer, and it's cleaner to do it *before* the first grant.
- A C-corp can grant ISOs (employees only) or NSOs, which is what candidates and investors expect.
- QSBS, the federal capital-gains break under IRC §1202, applies only to C-corp stock, so converting earlier can matter ([26 U.S.C. §1202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1202)).
- Many VC-track founders convert into a Delaware C-corp. Converting a *Georgia* LLC into a Delaware corp is possible but needs an attorney.
- After converting, the option basics apply:
  - a board-adopted equity plan, approved by shareholders within 12 months for ISOs ([26 U.S.C. §422](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/422));
  - a 409A valuation, from an independent appraisal no more than 12 months old at grant ([26 CFR 1.409A-1(b)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.409A-1), checked 2026-10-26);
  - a strike price at or above fair market value;
  - typically 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff.

**Path 2: Stay an LLC and grant a profits interest.**
A profits interest is a share of *future* growth. If it's structured to meet IRS Rev. Proc. 93-27 and 2001-43, the grant generally isn't taxable, and no 83(b) is needed for unvested interests ([Rev. Proc. 2001-43](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-irbs/irb01-34.pdf)). The catch is real for a first engineer:
- They become a **partner**, not an employee. They get a **K-1 instead of a W-2**, pay self-employment tax, and generally come off your normal payroll ([IRS Entities FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/small-business-self-employed-other-business/entities/entities-1)).
- That means quarterly estimated taxes for them and more complicated partnership returns for you. Many engineers find this confusing compared with options.
- "LLC unit options" exist, but there's no simple official guidance on them. Treat them as a tax-attorney question, not a DIY one.

**What I'd do next:**
1. Decide whether you're raising VC in the next 12–24 months. If yes, talk to a corporate attorney about converting before you promise equity in an offer letter.
2. Don't put a specific percentage or "options" in writing until the entity question is settled.
3. Whichever path you choose, get the engineer's PIIA (IP assignment) signed on day one.

**Bring in:** a corporate attorney (conversion, plan documents) and a CPA or tax attorney (profits interest structure, K-1 implications).
