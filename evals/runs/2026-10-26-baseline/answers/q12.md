**Short answer: yes, almost certainly too late. Talk to your startup attorney this week.** An 83(b) election "shall be filed not later than 30 days after the date the property was transferred" ([26 CFR 1.83-2(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.83-2), checked live 2026-10-26). The only extension is for a weekend or holiday: a postmark on the next business day still counts. Six weeks (about 42 days) is past that. There's no late-filing relief in the regulation, and the IRS doesn't grant one for a missed 83(b).

**First, confirm the actual transfer date.** The clock runs from when the shares were *issued to you* under the stock purchase agreement, not from the incorporation date. If your shares were issued later than incorporation, check whether you're inside 30 days. If you are, file today by certified mail using **Form 15620 (Rev. 4-2025)** or a written statement, and give the company a copy ([Form 15620](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f15620.pdf)).

**What missing it means.** Without an 83(b), each batch of shares is taxed as **ordinary income when it vests**, based on its value at that time minus what you paid. Because you're working for the company, that income is generally compensation, which can create payroll withholding obligations for the company. If the company's value rises, say after a seed round, every monthly vesting tranche becomes a growing tax bill with no cash to pay it. That's why founders file the 83(b) at a near-zero value.

**What attorneys commonly look at to fix it.** These are general practice, not from an official source, and every one of them has tax and cap-table consequences that need professional sign-off:
- **Cancel and reissue:** unwind the original grant and issue new restricted shares at current FMV, then file a new 83(b) within 30 days of the new issuance. This works best while the company is still worth close to nothing, which argues for moving fast.
- **Remove or restructure vesting:** if the shares aren't subject to vesting, Section 83 timing doesn't bite the same way. Investors will usually want founder vesting back later, though.
- **Do nothing and plan for it:** tax at each vest. This may be tolerable only if the company's value stays very low until you can restructure.

**Next steps:**
1. Pull the stock purchase agreements and find the exact issuance date.
2. Call the attorney who did your incorporation. Missed 83(b)s are a known issue, and they'll have a playbook.
3. Bring in a CPA to estimate the tax on each vesting tranche under each option.
4. Don't raise money or issue other equity until it's resolved. Value-setting events make every fix more expensive.
